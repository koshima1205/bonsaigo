*Keyaki* (*Zelkova serrata*) is a deciduous tree of the elm family. The Missouri Botanical Garden says it is native to Japan, Taiwan and eastern China, and in nature grows 50 to 80 feet (roughly 15 to 24 m) tall with an upward-branching, vase-shaped crown. In bonsai, that grand shape is reduced into a small pot.

## The broom style and the winter look
The classic zelkova form is the *hōki-dachi*, or broom style: a straight trunk that fans out into many fine branches, like an upside-down broom. Bonsai Empire calls the winter silhouette and its fine ramification the tree's nicest feature. For younger trees, branches are tied in bundles in autumn to encourage the broom shape, and the ties come off in spring before the buds open.

## Let it grow, then cut
The basic method is "grow, then cut back." Let a new shoot reach four to six leaves, then cut it to two. This multiplies shoots and makes branching finer. After leaf fall, prune thick twigs and overly long shoots.

## Defoliation: read the tree
Young trees can be defoliated in June to encourage fine branching. On older trees, remove only the larger leaves so light reaches the smaller inner ones. Do either only on a strong, healthy tree.

## Sun, water and winter
Give full sun in the growing season; in regions with very strong sun, use semi-shade during the hottest weeks. Water so the root ball never dries out completely, but avoid overwatering, which can cause root rot, and avoid very calcareous water. In winter, protect against freezing, especially in shallow pots, with a frost-free but cold place.

## Wiring and repotting
Wire in autumn, winter or early spring before the buds open, when no leaves hide the branch structure. Repot every two to three years and prune roots carefully. Shallow oval or rectangular pots suit it.

## Pests
The botanical garden says serious pests and diseases are few, but spider mites can be a problem in hot, dry summers.

## Knowing the tree
The Missouri Botanical Garden describes oblong leaves with coarse teeth, and fall color from yellow through orange to red-brown. Smooth gray bark exfoliates with age to reveal orange-brown inner bark. The tree is also noted for resistance to Dutch elm disease, and is valued as timber in Japan. That changing bark is part of the charm of an old zelkova.

## Summary
- Zelkova is loved for its winter twigs and broom style; bundle young branches in autumn
- Let shoots grow to four to six leaves, then cut back to two
- Defoliate only strong trees; protect from freezing in winter
