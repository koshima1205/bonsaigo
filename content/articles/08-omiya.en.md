In Omiya, in Saitama City, a village of bonsai artisans took shape. It is a place no history of bonsai can leave out.

## From Edo to Meiji
In the Edo period, daimyo gardens were built and gardening flourished among ordinary people too. After the Meiji era began, some garden-tree growers turned into bonsai specialists, and the number of bonsai artisans slowly grew.

## The 1923 earthquake
When the Great Kanto Earthquake struck in 1923, bonsai growers in Tokyo left the city in search of soil and water suited to bonsai. They settled in Omiya, north of Hikawa Shrine. Bonsai Empire notes that about thirty grower families resettled there, and that the place became the center of Japanese bonsai culture.

## The village's rules
The growers set rules for their own community: no two-story houses, and hedges instead of walls. This self-governing arrangement became Omiya Bonsai Village.

## The village today
At its peak the village had 30 bonsai gardens; at the time of the ministry's article, six remained. Each year from May 3 to 5, the Great Bonsai Festival offers special exhibitions of fine trees and sales.

## Omiya Bonsai Art Museum
In March 2010, the Saitama City Omiya Bonsai Art Museum opened. It is described as the world's first public bonsai museum, created for research on bonsai culture and sharing information. Together, the village and the museum make Omiya a destination for people around the world who want to learn about bonsai.

## A link to the wider world
In April 2017, the 8th World Bonsai Convention was held in Saitama City, the first in Japan in 28 years since the first convention there in 1989.

## Summary (3 lines)
- Growers moved to Omiya after the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake.
- They set village rules such as no two-story houses and hedges instead of walls.
- The world's first public bonsai museum opened there in 2010.
