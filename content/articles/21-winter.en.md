In winter the tree is dormant. Because growth has stopped, it suits big structural changes. Months are a guide for the Kanto plain in Japan (*Kanto heiya*); climate elsewhere shifts them.

## Winter is for structure
Bonsai Empire's calendar lists dormancy as the time for structural, replacement and maintenance pruning. In Japanese guidance, pruning and restyling of conifers (*shohakurui*, pines, junipers and similar) belongs to December and January. Cuts are made flat and sealed with a protective material.

## Old needles and bud count
On pines, remove last year's needles before pruning or wiring, so light reaches the inner canopy. An English guide leaves two terminal buds at each branch end. On broadleaves, clear fallen leaves to avoid trapped moisture.

## Wiring
Winter is a good time for wire, but species differ. A Japanese guide says black pine can be wired in winter and then kept under eaves to avoid freezing. Five-needle pine can be wired anytime in dormancy, though careful growers wait until March. Red pine splits easily, so wait until it is active. The guide also names one Japanese juniper that is better left unwired; if unsure about your tree, ask a craftsperson.

## Overwintering — cold, but never frozen
Pines are hardy, but container trees need winter protection. A guide says pines need several weeks of dormancy at about 0 to 7 °C (32 to 45 °F). Keep trees outdoors and avoid heated rooms, since warmth can wake buds too early.

The aim is to keep the soil from freezing and the tree from freezing through. A Japanese guide begins moving vulnerable trees into a storage space (*muro*) in late December in central and western Japan; colder regions go earlier, and mild regions may not need it. Before moving them in, let them feel a cold spell or two so they know it is winter. The guide also warns against over-protecting.

For junipers, protect once temperatures fall below about -10 °C (14 °F). Purplish-brown foliage on junipers in frost is normal and turns green again in spring.

## Winter watering and pots
Winter wind dries pots faster than expected, so keep watering. But cold soil dries slowly, and overwatering can harm roots. A pot full of water can crack when it freezes, so move vulnerable pots under cover.

## Other winter jobs
Deadwood carving (*jin* and *shari*, the pale bare wood) belongs to winter work. Kindai Bonsai calls December to January the best season for carving shimpaku wood: rough-cut first, then sand smooth. For a New Year display, ume can be moved indoors to a cool entrance from January to early February, with the buds misted.

## Summary (3 lines)
- Winter is dormant: good for structural pruning and wire, with species-specific cautions.
- Overwinter with cold but no freezing, and no heated rooms.
- Wind dries pots, so keep watering, and protect pots that could crack.

## A question for the craftsperson
How do you tell where "cold enough" ends and "frozen" begins when choosing a winter spot?
