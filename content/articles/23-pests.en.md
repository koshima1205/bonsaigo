The sooner you notice a pest or disease, the lighter the problem. This article gives general ways to recognize them and think about them. It does not name products or doses; if you are unsure whether something is a disease, ask an expert.

## First, doubt that it is a pest
Yellowing or dropping leaves are more often a watering or light problem than an insect. Bonsai Empire's troubleshooting guide says to check soil moisture and light first, and to remove only leaves that are fully yellow and come away easily. Do not fertilize a stressed tree and do not repot it immediately; steady its conditions first. Real pests usually leave visible clues: the insects themselves, webbing, sticky residue or damaged leaves.

## Look regularly
Check the tops and undersides of leaves, branches and new growth. Pests are tiny, so sticky leaves, discolored spots, fine webbing and distorted shoots are often the first sign.

## Aphids
Small, soft insects that suck sap from leaves and young shoots. They gather on new growth and can make leaves sticky. Warm, dry conditions and poor air circulation favor them. For an early infestation, gently rinsing the foliage is a suggested first step.

## Spider mites
Very small, and fond of hot, dry conditions. Look for fine pale speckling, dull leaf color and, in advanced cases, thin webbing among leaves or branches. The damage often shows before you see the mites. Raising humidity and improving airflow makes spread less likely.

## Scale insects
They appear as small, hard bumps on branches and leaves, feeding on sap and weakening a tree over time. Visible ones can be removed with a soft brush or cotton swab, then watched. Because eggs can survive under the shell, repeat checks are usually needed.

## Pine disease: leave it to experts
Pine wilt disease (*matsu zaisenchu-byo*, "pine wood nematode disease") kills pines suddenly. A Forestry Agency magazine feature by the Forestry Research Institute explains that the cause is a nematode, which a beetle called the Japanese pine sawyer carries from tree to tree. Researchers established this link in 1970. Control falls into two groups: dealing with dead trees, and prevention such as spraying or trunk injection, with resistant seedlings also used. It also notes that very hot summers in recent years have spread the damage.

That article covers forest pines, not potted bonsai. Still, if a pine suddenly changes color and dies back, do not diagnose it yourself; ask an expert, such as a bonsai nursery or a local office, early.

## Summary (3 lines)
- When symptoms appear, check water and light first, then look for insects, webbing or stickiness.
- Rinse aphids, raise humidity for mites, and remove scale by hand and keep watching.
- A pine that dies back suddenly is a job for an expert, not a guess.
