Watering and placement are the two parts of bonsai care that matter most, and that you will deal with almost every day.

## Watering: read the soil, not the clock
There is no fixed schedule. How much water a tree needs depends on its species, size, the pot, the season, the soil mix and your climate. A reliable guide is to water when the soil surface becomes slightly dry.

Beginners can push a finger about 1 cm (0.4 in) into the soil to check. With experience you will learn to judge dryness by eye.

## Water thoroughly
When you water, soak the whole root system until water runs from the drainage holes, then repeat a few minutes later so it soaks in. Use a can with a fine rose and pour gently from above. Rainwater is considered best, though tap water is acceptable.

## Watch out for too much, too
If the soil dries out completely, a tree can weaken or die. On the other hand, overwatering cuts off oxygen to the roots and can cause rot; it is generally considered more dangerous than underwatering. Aim for soil that is moist but well drained, with a brief drying between waterings.

Signs of too little water include drooping leaves, soil shrinking from the pot's edge, and leaf drop. A single missed watering is often harmless for a hardy tree whose soil was still slightly moist. Water well and watch it.

## Seasonal changes
Spring and summer need more water, especially at 24–35°C (75–95°F). In autumn and winter, growth slows and so does water use. Below about 10°C (50°F) the soil dries more slowly, and many trees need only occasional watering in winter dormancy. Misting the foliage barely affects humidity and is no substitute for proper watering.

## Placement: outdoors by default
Most bonsai trees should live outdoors all year, because following the seasons keeps them healthy. Overprotecting a tree in winter can weaken it.

Choose a bright, sunny place. Most bonsai need at least 4–6 hours of direct sun daily. Morning sun is especially valuable, while in hot climates some shade from strong afternoon sun helps. Some species tolerate part shade, but growth weakens if light is too low.

Indoors is mainly a temporary display spot. Even beside a window, light drops quickly a few feet away. Tropical and subtropical species, such as ficus, suit indoor life, and a drop below 10°C (50°F) is a signal to bring them inside. Trees from temperate climates should stay outdoors through winter.

## Summary (3 lines)
- Water when the soil is slightly dry, and soak until it drains.
- Avoid both overwatering and letting the soil dry out completely.
- Keep trees outdoors in good light, adjusting to your climate.
