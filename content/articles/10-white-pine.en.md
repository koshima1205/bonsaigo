Japanese white pine (*goyomatsu*, *Pinus parviflora*) takes its Japanese name from its needles, which come in bundles of five. The needles are soft and gather in dense tufts at the branch tips. A botanical garden profile notes that in the wild it can grow very tall, with a pyramid shape when young and a flatter, more spreading crown with age. In bonsai, people value its fine foliage and calm presence.

## The big difference from black pine: one flush
Black pine is cut back in *mekiri* to force a second flush. White pine grows only once a year (*ichido-nobi*), so you do not cut every candle. In fact, removing all of them can seriously weaken or even kill the tree.

## Evening out the candles
From late spring to early summer the new candles extend. Shorten the overly long ones so they match the rest, and where more than two emerge from one point, reduce them to two. The aim is to stop strong shoots from outrunning weaker areas. If you missed thinning in spring, you can still remove surplus shoots in autumn.

Even candle lengths let light reach the whole tree and keep the silhouette balanced. Pulling excess old needles in the stronger areas helps level out vigor in the same way.

## Sun and water
Grow it outdoors in full sun. The botanical garden notes it tolerates many soils as long as drainage is good, but prefers cool summers and dislikes heat and humidity. Overwatering damages roots, so water once the surface has dried. While new shoots are developing, avoid heavy rain on the tree if you can.

## Feeding and repotting
Healthy trees can be fed from spring through autumn. Repot in spring when the buds start to move. Late summer or early autumn also works, provided the tree has time to grow fine roots before winter.

## About the rootstock
White pines are often grafted onto black pine roots for steadier growth. If you buy one, take a look at the base to see where the graft sits — it tells you how the tree was made.

## Knowing the tree
The botanical garden profile describes slightly twisted green needles up to about 3 inches long, white on their inner surfaces. The oval cones, two to three inches long, can stay on the tree for up to seven years. The grayish-black bark is smooth on young trees and becomes fissured and scaly with age. Enjoying that aged bark is part of the appeal of a white pine in bonsai.

## Summary
- White pine grows once a year; never cut all the candles
- Even out candle lengths and keep at most two shoots per point
- Full sun outdoors, don't overwater, and mind summer heat and humidity
