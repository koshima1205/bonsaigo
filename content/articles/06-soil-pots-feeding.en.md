In a small pot, soil, container and feeding make up the tree's whole world. Here are the basics.

## What soil must do
Good bonsai soil needs three things: it holds enough water between waterings, it drains excess water quickly, and it keeps air pockets between particles so roots can breathe. Garden soil is discouraged because it hardens when dry and makes watering difficult.

## Common components
- **Akadama** (赤玉土, "red ball soil"): a hard-baked Japanese clay made for bonsai. Sift out the dust before use. It breaks down after about two years, reducing drainage.
- **Pumice** (軽石, *karuishi*): a soft volcanic rock that holds water and nutrients and encourages fine roots.
- **Lava rock**: holds some water and gives structure, though roots do not grow into it.
- **Fine gravel or grit**: improves drainage and aeration.

A typical mix, by volume, is 50% akadama, 25% pumice and 25% lava rock for deciduous trees, and equal thirds for pines and other conifers. If you cannot water often, use more akadama; in wet climates, add lava rock or grit. Consider replacing the soil when drainage slows or roots circle the pot.

## Choosing a pot
A pot needs drainage holes and holes for the wires that fix the tree in place. Traditional pots are stoneware or porcelain that does not absorb water, glazed or unglazed.

**Size.** A common guide is that the pot's depth roughly matches the trunk's width just above the surface roots. Oval and rectangular pots are often about two-thirds of the tree's height in length; round and square pots about one-third. Trees with big foliage may suit a wider pot. Maples, fruiting and flowering trees prefer deeper pots.

**Shape.** Match the pot to the tree's character. A thick, scarred, sparse and powerful tree suits a deep, angular pot with an outward-flaring rim ("masculine"). A smooth, graceful tree suits a low, softly curved pot with an inward-curving rim ("feminine"). Round and drum pots are fairly neutral.

**Color.** Echo a color in the tree: bark, leaf, flower or fruit. Brown, gray and earth tones are safe; blue or cool green can add contrast. This pairing of tree and pot is called *hachi-utsuri* (鉢映り). Rules can be broken, and personal taste matters.

## Feeding
In a pot a tree can reach only a limited amount of nutrients, so you replace them with fertilizer. The NPK ratio on the label matters more than whether it is organic or liquid; follow the instructions. Solid organic fertilizer is placed on the soil as *oki-goe* (置き肥, "placed fertilizer") in small cups or baskets so it does not wash away or get eaten by birds. Liquid fertilizer is given while watering.

Feed during the growing season, from early spring to mid- or late autumn. One scheme uses higher nitrogen in spring (about 10:6:6), balanced in summer (6:6:6) and lower nitrogen in autumn (3:6:6). Many growers now prefer a balanced feed all season, tapering off at the end. Do not feed outdoor trees in winter.

Too much fertilizer can cause weak, rapid growth, oversized leaves, root burn and brown, falling leaves. If it happens, flush the soil with clean water and pause feeding.

## Summary (3 lines)
- Think of soil in terms of water retention, drainage and air.
- Match pot depth, shape and color to the tree (*hachi-utsuri*).
- Feed in the growing season, and err on the light side.
